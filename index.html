What it is

Not a smart device

Taps is a heavy, hand-cast puck — concrete and steel BBs, no battery — that sits at a threshold in your day and holds one of two states. You flip it by hand. Nothing lights up. Nothing syncs. The only intelligence in the system is the one deciding to turn it over.

The test isn't whether it's useful. It's whether it earns a permanent place — whether removing it leaves a gap you reach for before you remember why.

The object changes because the human changed it.
The display only reveals the state.

That's the whole filter. Any future version — e-ink, sensors, anything — has to hold this line, or it becomes a screen wearing a rock's face.

Why it works

Grounding, not gamification

A physical flip is a small act of embodied cognition — the hand does something the mind can register as closure. That's the same mechanism behind a lot of quiet regulation tools: a light switch, a door lock check, a worry stone. The object doesn't do the regulating. It gives the moment a shape you can complete.

Placing it at a threshold — the door, the desk, the bed — borrows from how habits actually form: tied to a location and a transition, not a reminder or a willpower decision. You're not being told to focus or rest. You're marking that you already decided to.

This isn't a treatment and it doesn't replace one. It's a low-stakes way to add a small, repeatable moment of agency to an ordinary transition — useful on its own terms, whether or not anything is "wrong" to begin with.

The 14-day log

Track the gap, not the novelty

For each puck: did you flip it, ignore it, or reach for it when it wasn't there? Tap to log today's entry — this stays on this device, no account, no sync.

Flipped = you turned it over. Reached = you noticed its absence or wanted it and it wasn't there. Counts reset only if you clear them.

Before you flip

Reset

The puck asks for one decision, not a stream of them. This is the same idea in your body: one slow cycle of breath, long enough to move you from reacting to choosing, before you touch the object.

HOLD
0 cycles

In for 4. Hold for 4. Out for 6. The exhale is longer than the inhale on purpose — it's the part that tells your nervous system the moment has passed and you're safe to act deliberately, not reflexively.

Run it for three to five cycles. Then flip the puck, or don't. Either way, you'll be choosing instead of reaching.
